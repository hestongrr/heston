What Does a Geotechnical Engineer Do in Nevada?

July 29, 2026 · Heston Norcott, E.I.

If you build anything in Nevada — a solar farm, a subdivision, a strip mall, a race track — the first person you need is a geotechnical engineer. Before the concrete is poured, before the steel is framed, someone has to answer the fundamental question: What is this ground actually going to do?

I've been answering that question for over a decade. Here's a look at what the job actually looks like on the ground in the Silver State.

The Geology We Work With

Nevada is basin and range country. That means the valleys — where almost everything gets built — are filled with thousands of feet of alluvial deposits: gravels, sands, silts, and clays washed down from the surrounding mountain ranges. In Las Vegas, you also deal with caliche (calcium-carbonate cemented layers that can be rock-hard or misleadingly soft), variable groundwater, and occasionally expansive soils that can lift a foundation if nobody catches them.

Understanding this variability — and knowing when to flag a risk — is what separates a useful geotechnical report from a generic one.

The Day-to-Day Work

Geotechnical engineering in Nevada breaks into three main buckets:

  • Site investigation. Drilling borings, sampling soil and rock, running SPT tests, installing piezometers. You get your boots dirty. I've supervised drilling crews from Laughlin to Ely, and there's no substitute for seeing the spoil pile in person.
  • Laboratory testing. Sieve analysis, Atterberg limits, Proctor compaction, expansion index, direct shear, consolidation. The numbers in the report come from here.
  • Analysis and recommendations. Slope stability, bearing capacity, settlement, liquefaction potential, earth retention. This is where the engineering judgment kicks in — and where tools like Oasys, Python scripts, and finite-difference models save hours of manual calculation.

Slope Stability in the Basin and Range

Some of the most technically interesting work I've done involves slope stability. When you're building on or near the alluvial fans at the base of Nevada's mountain ranges, you're dealing with heterogeneous deposits, potentially high groundwater, and seismic considerations. The analysis has to account for all of it — static and pseudo-static factors of safety, critical failure surfaces, reinforcement requirements.

To show what this looks like in practice, I built a free interactive slope stability calculator — run an infinite slope, planar wedge, or Bishop circular analysis on any geometry and water table, live in your browser. It's the same family of methods used on Nevada cut slopes, minus the $5,000/year license.

Why It Matters

A bad geotechnical recommendation can crack a foundation, close a road, or worse. A good one is invisible — the building settles evenly, the slope stays put, the pavement doesn't crack. That's the goal: make the ground a non-issue for everyone else building on top of it.
