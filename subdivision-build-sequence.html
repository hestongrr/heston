Why this exists

The part nobody films

A finished subdivision looks like it was placed on the ground. It wasn't — the ground was rebuilt to receive it. On a typical Las Vegas valley tract, months of work happen before a single stick of lumber arrives, and essentially all of it is invisible by the time anyone moves in.

That invisible work is where the geotechnical engineering lives, and it is the reason the house stays flat. Native desert soils in this valley are frequently loose, collapsible when first wetted, and interrupted by caliche — a naturally cemented layer that can be strong enough to need a hoe ram in one spot and absent thirty feet away. You do not build on that as found. You take it apart and put it back under control.

The model above walks through that sequence. The dimensions come from ordinary residential practice: 50 × 100 ft lots, a 40 ft street corridor at a 2% crown, pads set roughly a foot above the gutter for drainage, and 3 ft of remedial overexcavation below pad grade. Cut and fill volumes are computed from the actual mesh, not typed in.

What's real and what's representative. The engineering standards, dimensions, sequence, and soil profile are drawn from ordinary Las Vegas valley residential practice, and the earthwork quantities are computed from the model geometry. The terrain itself is a representative alluvial-fan surface rather than a specific parcel, and no project-specific data from any client report is used here. Swapping in a real USGS elevation tile and a recorded subdivision map is straightforward — that's the next version.

The sequence, and why the order is fixed

Most of this ordering isn't preference. It's forced by gravity, by access, and by what you're willing to dig back up.

#PhaseWhy it has to come here
0InvestigationBorings before design — you can't set pad grades until you know what's under them
1Clearing & grubbingOrganics can't be left under fill; they decompose and the surface settles
2Rough gradingBalance cut and fill on site — hauling dirt off or in is expensive
3Remedial gradingOverexcavate and recompact before anything is built on top of it
4Wet utilitiesSewer runs on gravity, so it's deepest and goes in first
5Dry utilitiesShallower, and can dodge the wet lines already in the ground
6StreetsAfter the trenches, or you'd cut up new pavement
7Pad certificationCompaction and grade signed off before a foundation is permitted
8FoundationsPost-tensioned slabs are common here — they tolerate soil movement
9Vertical constructionThe part everyone photographs
10Final grade & flatworkDrainage away from the house is the last defense, and the most often broken
