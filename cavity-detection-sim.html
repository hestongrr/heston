The physics — what's happening at the cavity

A seismic survey puts three kinds of elastic wave into the ground — and the cavity treats each one differently. In the wavefield above, each is colored by the operator that defines it, so you can watch them separate and label themselves.

Body wave · red

P-wave · compressional

Particle motion parallel to travel — push-pull. Fastest of the three, and the only one that can move through fluids and air. It can enter the void, but the huge impedance jump reflects almost all of it. Isolated here by wavefield divergence.

Vp = √((λ + 2μ) / ρ)
Body wave · blue

S-wave · shear

Particle motion perpendicular to travel. Needs shear stiffness to exist — and air has none (μ = 0). So an S-wave cannot travel through the cavity at all: it reflects and mode-converts at the wall. Isolated here by wavefield curl.

Vs = √(μ / ρ)  →  μ=0 in air  →  Vs = 0
Surface wave · violet

Rayleigh wave

A coupled P–SV motion bound to the free surface — retrograde ellipse, energy within about one wavelength of the top. It carries most of the source energy, so a shallow void sitting in that band scatters it strongly. Shows as red+blue mixed near the surface.

VR ≈ 0.92 · Vs  (for ν = 0.25)

So — inside the void?

  • S-waves: nothing. Air can't carry shear, so no shear wave exists inside — it reflects and mode-converts at the boundary.
  • P-waves: a faint acoustic rattle. Some compressional energy leaks in at ~340 m/s, but the ≈1000:1 impedance mismatch (soil vs air) reflects almost all of it.
  • The real action is at the wall: the void acts as a scatterer — it back-scatters energy toward the source, diffracts around itself, and casts an amplitude shadow beyond it. That anomaly is the detection signal.
  • Mode conversion: P ↔ SV swap at the boundary, and Rayleigh energy leaks downward into body waves. The high-velocity layer beneath then reflects energy back up — the interference that made the interpretation hard, and why overtone-image stacking was needed.
Note on this model This browser demo solves the 2-D elastic (P-SV) wave equation on a staggered velocity–stress grid, so it genuinely produces P, S, and Rayleigh waves and their mode conversion — colored by divergence (P) and curl (S). It's a 2nd-order isotropic teaching version; the thesis used LLNL's E3D / WPP for full 3-D elastic modeling. Knowing exactly where a model's edges are is the whole job.

Why build it to teach? Over a decade of leading field crews and mentoring junior engineers, the teams that moved fastest were the ones who could see the physics, not just run the software. A good engineer solves the problem; a good lead makes the next person able to solve it too. That's the standard I build to.
