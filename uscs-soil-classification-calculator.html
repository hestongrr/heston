    How USCS classification works

    The Unified Soil Classification System (ASTM D2487) sorts soil by grain size and plasticity into a two-letter group symbol. The arithmetic is trivial; the classification is a decision tree, and most errors are wrong turns rather than bad math.

    1. Coarse-grained or fine-grained?

    The split is 50 % passing the No. 200 sieve. Pass 50 % or more and the soil is fine-grained — you're on the plasticity chart. Less than 50 % and it's coarse-grained.

    2. Gravel or sand?

    For coarse soils, the No. 4 sieve decides — but the split is on the coarse fraction, not the total sample. More than half of the coarse fraction retained on the No. 4 makes it gravel (G); otherwise sand (S). Getting this backwards is the single most common error.

    3. How much fines?

    Fines (% passing #200)Result
    < 5 %Clean — grade it: GW/GP/SW/SP
    5 – 12 %Dual symbol — e.g. SW-SM
    > 12 %Fines govern: GM/GC/SM/SC

    4. Well graded or poorly graded?

    Two coefficients, and the thresholds differ between gravel and sand:

    Cu = D₆₀ / D₁₀    Cc = D₃₀² / (D₆₀ × D₁₀)

    Well graded requires
    GravelCu ≥ 4 and 1 ≤ Cc ≤ 3
    SandCu ≥ 6 and 1 ≤ Cc ≤ 3

    Most people memorise one number. Fail either condition and it's poorly graded.

    5. The plasticity chart

    For fine-grained soils, plot LL against PI. The A-line separates clays from silts; LL = 50 separates low from high plasticity.

    A-line: PI = 0.73 (LL − 20)    U-line: PI = 0.9 (LL − 8)

    The U-line isn't a classification boundary — it's an upper bound on real soil. If your point plots above it, check the lab data rather than reaching for an exotic explanation.
