OSHA 29 CFR 1926 — Subpart P (Excavations)

Sand is Type C soil — cohesionless and granular. OSHA's maximum allowable slope for Type C is 1.5H:1V, or 34°. That number is not a guess: it is sand's natural angle of repose. The yellow guide line in the scene is the 1.5H:1V line drawn from the pile crest.

  • 0-4 ft deep — inspect the site; slopes can be temporary.
  • >4 ft deep — egress ladder/ramp required within 25 ft (1926.651).
  • >5 ft deep — protection required: shoring, trench shields, or 1.5H:1V sloping (1926.652). No exceptions for "dry, stable" sand.
  • Spoil piles — keep at least 2 ft back from the edge; it's dead load pushing on the trench wall.
  • Competent person must inspect daily, before entry, and after rain (1926.651).

Silica & PPE

Respirable crystalline silica (29 CFR 1926.1153) is a real killer in this business — it causes silicosis, COPD, and lung cancer. Cutting, sawing, or dry-grinding sand-rich material (concrete, masonry, sandstone) produces it. The rule: wet methods, local exhaust, or a respirator. This simulation is the only place you should play with dry sand.

Hard hats, high-vis, and steel toes are the minimum in any active excavation (1926.95). Nobody plans to get crushed by a spoil pile collapse — they just don't plan for it.

Why does it pile?

Dry sand can't stand steeper than its angle of repose — the steepest slope grains hold without sliding. Drag the slider: loose sand (~30°) slides flat fast, dense sand (~38°) stands steeper and mobilizes strength. Dense sand behaves more like a “soil” than loose sand — that's effective stress in action.

Try it: pour a pile, then drag the slider from dense to loose. The pile sheds material until it hits the new repose angle — exactly what a real spoil pile does.
