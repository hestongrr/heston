What AI is actually good at in engineering

An honest map of where it saves a working engineer hours, where it will hand you a wrong answer with complete confidence, and the one habit that makes the difference. Including real errors it produced in my own published work — and how each one got caught.

Heston Norcott · Geotechnical Engineer, E.I. · Las Vegas, NV
Written 2026 · Free · No signup

Most of what engineers read about AI is written by people who have never had to stamp anything, defend a number in a design review, or explain to a contractor why the pad elevation changed. The advice ends up in one of two ditches: it either promises the work is about to do itself, or it warns you off entirely.

Neither is useful, because the actual situation is more specific and more interesting than either. AI is genuinely excellent at a well-defined set of things in engineering practice, and genuinely dangerous at another set, and the boundary between them is learnable. That boundary is what this lesson is about.

I use these tools daily on real geotechnical work — classification routines, numerical solvers, figures, report drafting, code for the tools published on this site. I am also studying for the PE Geotechnical exam, which means I am checking a lot of output against first principles right now. What follows is what that experience actually taught me, errors included.

1. The one thing to understand first

Engineers usually arrive expecting the failure mode to be stupidity — that the tool will say something obviously wrong and you will catch it because it is obviously wrong. That is not the failure mode.

The core idea

These systems are extraordinarily good at producing output that is plausible. Plausible is not the same as correct, and in engineering the gap between those two words is where people get hurt.

A wrong answer that looks wrong costs you nothing. A wrong answer that looks exactly like a right answer — correct units, reasonable magnitude, properly formatted, citing a real standard — is the one that reaches a drawing.

Here is the useful reframe: this is not a new problem. Our profession has always dealt with confidently-presented information of uncertain quality. A lab result can be wrong. A boring log can be mislabeled. A contractor's as-built can be fiction. A textbook can have a typo in an equation, and vendor software can have a bug in a module nobody exercises.

Engineering already has the answer to "plausible but possibly wrong." It is verification. Check the magnitude. Check the units. Check the limiting case. Ask whether the sign makes physical sense. Compare against a hand calculation. That discipline is not something you need to acquire to use AI safely — it is the thing you were trained to do, pointed at a new source.

Which leads to the rule that organizes everything below:

The rule

AI is safe in direct proportion to how cheaply you can check its work.

Where verification is fast, obvious, or automatic, use it aggressively — the speedup is real and large. Where verification is slow, subtle, or impossible, that is exactly where it will cost you more than it saves.

2. Where it genuinely saves you hours

These are the cases where a wrong answer announces itself immediately — the code throws an error, the figure looks wrong, the draft reads wrong. The cost of a mistake is close to zero, so you can move fast.

Strong — use freely

Writing code that computes something you already understand

This is the single biggest win available to a working engineer, and it is why Track 2 of this library exists. If you can state the calculation, you can get working code for it in minutes — sieve analysis, settlement over layers, a pressure diagram, a data conversion, a one-off script that saves an afternoon of spreadsheet work.

Verification is nearly free here: run it against a problem you already solved by hand. If the numbers match, the code works. That single check catches essentially everything.

Strong — use freely

Explaining an unfamiliar concept until it clicks

Asking the same question five different ways, at five levels of detail, without an ounce of impatience, is something no textbook and few colleagues can offer. For exam study this is close to unreasonable — "explain why the #4 split is taken on the coarse fraction, not total sample" gets a clear answer instantly.

Verify by closing the loop with a real source: the standard, the textbook, the code provision. Use it to understand, then confirm the fact.

Strong — use freely

First drafts of anything you were going to rewrite anyway

Report boilerplate, a scope of work, an email explaining a recommendation to a client, a proposal outline. You are going to edit it heavily regardless, and editing a mediocre draft is faster than facing a blank page.

Verification is automatic — you read every word before it goes out, because your name is on it.

Strong — use freely

Finding your own mistake

Underrated and excellent. Paste in a calculation, a script, or a paragraph and ask what is wrong with it. You are not accepting an answer — you are getting a list of candidates to check yourself. False positives cost you thirty seconds; a real catch saves you far more.

3. Where it will hurt you

Same tool, inverted economics. In these cases a wrong answer looks identical to a right one, and nothing downstream will flag it.

Avoid

Specific numerical values from codes and standards

Load factors, resistance factors, table lookups, section numbers, minimum embedments. It will produce a value with the right shape and the right units and cite a plausible section number, and you have no way to tell a correct recall from a confident reconstruction without opening the document.

And if you are opening the document anyway, you have not saved anything. Open the standard. Always.

Avoid

Engineering judgment on a specific site

"Is this slope stable?" "Is this bearing pressure appropriate?" It has not seen your borings, walked the site, or read the local geology. Southern Nevada caliche alone breaks the general case badly — it can be a competent bearing layer or a cemented crust with soft material underneath, and nothing in a general model knows which one you are standing on.

It also cannot be held responsible for the answer. You can.

Avoid

Anything where a subtle error stays invisible

Long calculation chains where an early wrong assumption propagates quietly. A sign convention that flips halfway through. A unit conversion buried in step six of twelve. If you cannot cheaply check the intermediate values, do not hand off the chain — hand off one step at a time and check each one.

Avoid

Client data you are not permitted to send out

A practical one that has nothing to do with accuracy. Unpublished reports, confidential site data, anything under an NDA — know your firm's policy before pasting. If the work needs to stay in-house, run a model locally instead. That is a solved problem and it is Lesson 3.5.

4. The middle: useful, but only with verification

This is where most real engineering work actually lands, and where the skill is.

TaskWhy it's worth doingWhat you must check
Technical figures & diagrams Enormous time savings versus drawing by hand The geometry, every time. See the soil nails below.
Numerical methods Gets a working solver up in an hour instead of a week Stability and convergence — the scheme can be subtly wrong and still run
Literature and background Fast orientation in an unfamiliar subject Every citation. Confirm the paper exists and says what was claimed.
Data cleanup & conversion Turns a tedious afternoon into ten minutes Spot-check rows against the source; confirm the record count
Report review Catches things you have read past five times Treat output as candidates to investigate, not corrections to accept

Notice the pattern: in every row the verification is specific. Not "review the output" — that is too vague to actually perform. Know the particular thing that goes wrong in that particular task, and check that.

5. Four real errors from my own published work

This is the part I think is worth the most, because it is the part nobody publishes. These are genuine errors that appeared in figures and code I built with AI assistance for this site. Every one of them got caught before it shipped, and how each got caught is the actual lesson.

The soil nails pointed the wrong way

Caught by: looking at the picture

A hillside stabilization figure came out with the soil nails drilled into the downhill face — into the mass that is trying to slide away, rather than anchored back into the stable ground behind the failure surface. The rendering was clean and professional. The dimensions were right. The engineering was backwards.

No amount of checking the code would have found this. It took one look by somebody who knows what a soil nail is for. Every figure gets eyeballed by an engineer before it ships, no matter how good it looks.

The uplift pressure came out negative

Caught by: the sign was physically impossible

A flow-net practice problem produced a negative uplift pressure under a structure. Water does not pull down on a base. The arithmetic was internally consistent — the error was an incorrectly placed datum, which propagated cleanly through every subsequent step and produced a confidently wrong final number.

Re-setting the datum at bedrock fixed it: 72.6 kPa at the heel down to 5.9 kPa at the toe. This is the classic case — the physics caught it, not the math. Asking "can this quantity even be negative?" is a two-second check that would have saved the whole chain.

The building was founded on a cliff face

Caught by: my own eyes, laughing

A 3-D site model built on real USGS terrain placed the structure halfway up a slope with the foundation hanging out into open air. Real elevation data, correct coordinate handling, and a building that could not exist.

The fix was to search the terrain for the flattest viable footprint and add an engineered fill skirt — which is, notably, exactly what you would actually do on that site. Correct input data does not produce a sensible result on its own. Nothing in the pipeline knew that buildings need ground underneath them.

The solver returned NaN — and this is the scary one

Caught by: the output was garbage. It might not have been.

Solving Laplace's equation for a flow net, the relaxation scheme used an over-relaxation factor of 1.92 applied with a Jacobi-style update. That combination is unstable, and it diverged to NaN. The fix is red-black Gauss–Seidel ordering, where over-relaxation is valid.

It got caught instantly because NaN is unmistakable. That is luck, and it is worth sitting with. A slightly less aggressive factor would not have blown up — it would have returned smooth, plausible, professionally-plotted contours that were quietly wrong, and I would have had no reason to look twice. Numerical methods fail silently far more often than they fail loudly.

This is why the verification for a solver is never "did it run." It is: does it reproduce a case with a known analytical answer?

A fifth one, different in kind

Worth separating out because it is not a hallucination at all. I recommended a professional community based on confirming that it existed. It existed. It was also completely dead — no meaningful activity, useful to nobody.

The answer was factually correct and practically worthless, because I had verified the wrong property. Existence was easy to check, so that is what got checked; liveliness was what actually mattered. This failure mode is subtle and common, and it is entirely on the engineer: make sure the thing you are verifying is the thing that matters.

6. The habit that makes all of this safe

Everything above collapses into one practice, and it takes about thirty seconds per result.

  1. Can this quantity be this sign? Negative uplift, negative settlement, a factor of safety below zero — the fastest check there is, and it catches propagated errors that look fine everywhere else.
  2. Is the order of magnitude right? You know roughly what a bearing pressure, a settlement, or a friction angle should be. Trust that instinct — it is the most valuable thing your experience gave you.
  3. Do the units work? All the way through, not just at the end.
  4. What happens at the limits? Set the cohesion to zero. Make the layer infinitely thick. Drop the water table below the footing. Does the result move the way the physics says it must?
  5. Does one hand calculation agree? One. Not the whole set. If a single worked case matches, the method is probably sound; if it does not, you have found something.
  6. Look at the picture. If it produced a figure, actually look at it, as an engineer. Three of the five errors above were visible on sight.
The reframe worth keeping

These checks are not overhead imposed by using AI. They are engineering. You would run them on a junior's calculation, on a lab report with a number that looked off, on output from software you had not used before.

Which points at something genuinely encouraging: the engineers best positioned to use these tools well are the ones with the strongest fundamentals — because verification is the skill, and they already have it. Judgment does not become less valuable here. It becomes the entire point.

7. What does not change

The engineer is responsible for the work. Not the tool, not the vendor, not the model.

That is not a caveat tacked onto the end — it is the reason the verification habit is non-negotiable rather than a nice practice. When a number ends up on a drawing, a person's judgment stands behind it, and that has not moved an inch. The tools change how fast you get to a candidate answer. They change nothing about who owns it.

Which is, honestly, the optimistic read. The part of this profession that is genuinely hard — knowing what matters on a site, what to be suspicious of, what the ground is likely doing between two borings — is exactly the part that stays yours.
